Project 01 — Perception & Safety

Emergency Landing Zone Selection for UAVs

A UAV that loses power mid-flight has seconds to pick a landing spot. Most vision pipelines answer "what's down there" and stop — they'll call a spot safe because a segmentation model was confident, even when that confidence is wrong. I built a pipeline that treats its own uncertainty as a first-class signal instead of noise to average away.

The failure case I designed around isn't "the model is wrong" — it's "the model is wrong and sure of it." That's the case that kills people.

The system fuses three channels: semantic segmentation (SegFormer-B2, fine-tuned on UAVid) to classify terrain, epistemic uncertainty via MC Dropout to flag where the model is guessing, and temporal/label disagreement across frames to catch spots the model flip-flops on. A candidate zone only qualifies as "safe" when all three agree — confident, consistent classification with low model uncertainty.

Tested on UAVid and Aeroscapes, the uncertainty-aware pipeline produced zero false safe-zone selections, against a baseline that confidently selected unsafe terrain when segmentation alone was wrong. I'm now extending it into closed-loop simulation with PX4 SITL and Gazebo — early stage, not yet flying autonomously, but the goal is to close the loop from perception to actual descent commands.

Project 02 — Powertrain & Mechanical Design

Vulcan — Baja SAE Drivetrain

As Powertrain Engineer and Electrical Lead on our Baja SAE team, I designed the drivetrain for Vulcan, our off-road car: a 4:1 spur gear reduction feeding a chain-and-sprocket final drive, sized to survive the abuse of an off-road endurance event.

The first version of the gearbox I designed worked on paper — and got rejected by the team. It was too bulky and too hard to actually manufacture with the tooling we had. That rejection was the more useful design review: I went back and cut the stage count, moving to a simpler chain-sprocket final drive that solved weight, packaging, and manufacturability at the same time instead of trading one for another.

A design that only works in SolidWorks isn't done. The redesign had to survive our own shop floor, not just an FEA report.

The final gearbox was validated in ANSYS FEA under 90 Nm peak torque, and I built a microcontroller-based safety interlock system to meet SAE E-Baja compliance. Coordinating a 7-person team through fabrication and vendor delays meant the car got built and running — we missed our target competition date because of vendor lead times outside our control, but the drivetrain itself held up.

Project 03 — Physiological Signal Processing

Real-Time ECG Peak Detection

At CSIR-CEERI, my contribution to a larger sensor-fusion system was the physiological monitoring pipeline: cleaning raw ECG, detecting heartbeats in real time, and deriving heart-rate variability (HRV) from them. The intended application is need-to-know, so I'm not sharing that context, the team's data, or the system's results here.

What I can show is the algorithm work itself, run against public ECG data. I wrote a custom QRS (heartbeat) detector, an extended version of the classic Pan-Tompkins method, and benchmarked it against NeuroKit2, a widely used open-source ECG library, on the MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database.

NeuroKit2 would occasionally "detect" a heartbeat in a flat, noisy stretch of signal where there wasn't one, nowhere near any real QRS complex. Pan-Tompkins++ didn't take the bait in the same spots.

On the MIT-BIH NSTDB benchmark, Pan-Tompkins++ hit 98.8% R-peak detection accuracy versus 81.7% for NeuroKit2 — a 17-point gap. The plots below show five representative cases: NeuroKit2 places a peak mid-baseline, off by 700–800 ms from the nearest real beat, while Pan-Tompkins++ stays correctly silent or locks onto the true QRS complex.
