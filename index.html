Project 01 · Perception & Safety

Emergency Landing Zone Selection for UAVs

A UAV that loses power mid-flight has seconds to pick a landing spot. Most vision pipelines answer "what's down there" and stop. They'll call a spot safe because a segmentation model was confident, even when that confidence is wrong. I built a pipeline that treats its own uncertainty as a first-class signal instead of noise to average away.

The failure case I designed around isn't "the model is wrong." It's "the model is wrong and sure of it." That's the case that kills people.

The system fuses three channels: semantic segmentation (SegFormer-B2, fine-tuned on UAVid) to classify terrain, epistemic uncertainty via MC Dropout to flag where the model is guessing, and temporal/label disagreement across frames to catch spots the model flip-flops on. A candidate zone only qualifies as "safe" when all three agree: confident, consistent classification with low model uncertainty.

Tested on UAVid and Aeroscapes, the uncertainty-aware pipeline produced zero false safe-zone selections, against a baseline that confidently selected unsafe terrain when segmentation alone was wrong. I'm now extending it into closed-loop simulation with PX4 SITL and Gazebo. It's early and not flying autonomously yet. The goal is to close the loop from perception to actual descent commands.

Project 02 · Thrust Vector Control

TVC Gimbal and Roll Vanes for an EDF Hopper (In Progress)

The first gimbal I designed had its linkage tabs hanging under the fan. Then I worked out where the exhaust goes at full tilt. The plume ran straight through the tabs and servos, right when the controller would be working hardest. I flipped the tabs up and moved both servos above the rings, beside the intake.

This is the engine module for a self-landing vehicle I'm building on a ₹20,000 budget: a two-axis gimbal designed to swing a full 70 mm electric ducted fan ±10° on both axes. Nested rings carry the fan, driven by two MG996R servos through bent steel-wire pushrods at roughly 3:1, so servo slop shrinks by the same factor at the fan.

A two-axis gimbal can't control roll, and a single fan spins the vehicle with its own reaction torque. So two NACA 0012 vanes sit in the jet, each driven directly by its own MG90S and deflected in opposite directions. My first vane was a flat plate across the whole duct. I trimmed the span to where the jet actually is, since the inner section sat in dead air behind the motor hub and the outer section was mostly outside the jet, and switched to an airfoil because a flat plate stalls early at this Reynolds number (about 70,000).

CAD caught the problems before the printer did. The pitch pushrod jammed the yaw axis, so its tab hole became an hourglass that lets the rod tilt without adding play. An 11-configuration interference study then found two collisions: the tab corners swing outward at tilt. Thinning both tabs from 5.5 to 4.0 mm left about 1.5 mm of clearance at 10°.

The servo cradles are printed into the mount, and the whole module connects through four lugs: a reusable test stand now, the airframe later. I've bench-tested servo actuation on an ESP32. Nothing is printed or flying yet. Next is printing, then a live demo of the gimbal and vanes following a hand-held IMU, then a load-cell stand to measure thrust and roll authority.

Project 03 · Powertrain & Mechanical Design

Vulcan: Baja SAE Drivetrain

As Powertrain Engineer and Electrical Lead on our Baja SAE team, I designed the drivetrain for Vulcan, our off-road car: a 4:1 spur gear reduction feeding a chain-and-sprocket final drive, sized to survive the abuse of an off-road endurance event.

The first version of the gearbox I designed worked on paper and got rejected by the team. It was too bulky and too hard to actually manufacture with the tooling we had. That rejection was the more useful design review: I went back and cut the stage count, moving to a simpler chain-sprocket final drive that solved weight, packaging, and manufacturability at the same time instead of trading one for another.

A design that only works in SolidWorks isn't done. The redesign had to survive our own shop floor, not just an FEA report.

The final gearbox was validated in ANSYS FEA under 90 Nm peak torque, and I built a microcontroller-based safety interlock system to meet SAE E-Baja compliance. Coordinating a 7-person team through fabrication and vendor delays meant the car got built and running. We still missed competition because of vendor lead times outside our control, but the drivetrain itself held up.

Project 04 · Computational Fluid Dynamics

Ground-Effect Wing Aerodynamics (In Progress)

For my Aerodynamics coursework, I'm running a ground-effect study on an inverted single-element wing, framed around F1-style front/rear wing aerodynamics: how downforce and the flow separation point change with ride height above the ground plane.

Before running that geometry, I'm validating my CFD workflow on a known airfoil, the NACA 6412, in ANSYS Fluent. The plots below are from that validation sweep: mesh and contours for turbulent viscosity ratio, static pressure, and velocity magnitude, plus the lift and drag coefficients I'm logging across the angle-of-attack range.

This part isn't the headline result, it's me making sure the mesh, solver settings, and convergence behavior are trustworthy before I trust them on the actual wing geometry.

The sweep ran from −12° to +4° on the inverted wing. In the linear range the lift slope came out at 0.101 per degree, and the zero-lift angle landed within 0.3° of thin-airfoil theory. Peak L/D was 62. It stalls between −10° and −12°, where steady RANS stops being trustworthy, so I'm not leaning on the post-stall numbers. Wall y+ stays within the viscous sublayer across the surface. Drag runs higher than XFOIL, which is expected: SST treats the whole boundary layer as turbulent. The ground-effect ride-height study is next.

Project 05 · Physiological Signal Processing

Real-Time ECG Peak Detection

At CSIR-CEERI, my contribution to a larger sensor-fusion system was the physiological monitoring pipeline: cleaning raw ECG, detecting heartbeats in real time, and deriving heart-rate variability (HRV) from them. The intended application is need-to-know, so I'm not sharing that context, the team's data, or the system's results here.

What I can show is the algorithm work itself, run against public ECG data. I wrote a custom QRS (heartbeat) detector, an extended version of the classic Pan-Tompkins method, and benchmarked it against NeuroKit2, a widely used open-source ECG library, on the MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database.

NeuroKit2 would occasionally "detect" a heartbeat in a flat, noisy stretch of signal where there wasn't one, nowhere near any real QRS complex. Pan-Tompkins++ didn't take the bait in the same spots.

On the MIT-BIH NSTDB benchmark at 24 dB and 18 dB SNR, Pan-Tompkins++ hit 98.8% R-peak detection accuracy versus 81.7% for NeuroKit2. The plots below show five representative cases: NeuroKit2 places a peak mid-baseline, off by 700–800 ms from the nearest real beat, while Pan-Tompkins++ stays correctly silent or locks onto the true QRS complex.

Project 06 · Computer Vision & Validation

Image Stitching for Dimensional Inspection

A 300 mm part photographed far enough back to fit the frame gives too few pixels per millimetre to measure anything. The alternative is several close-up photographs that overlap, joined afterwards. I built the system that joins them, from first principles on top of OpenCV primitives rather than a ready-made stitching call.

The hard part isn't the joining. It's knowing whether the join is correct. A wrong alignment produces a clean-looking composite, and somebody reads a false dimension off it.

The pipeline finds ORB features in each photograph, filters matches with Lowe's ratio test and a mutual consistency check, then fits homography and similarity models with RANSAC and MAGSAC++. The decision I'd single out: candidate alignments are ranked by whether the overlapping pixels actually agree, not by how many matches support them. In one real case the correct alignment had 19 supporting matches and a wrong one had 25. Counting matches picks the wrong answer. Checking pixels picks the right one.

When a stitched 3D-printed box showed two holes where it has one, I didn't tune parameters until it looked better. I split the matches by surface, 113 on the part and 390 on the background, and fitted a model to each. Fitted to the background, the error was 2.2 px there and 179 px on the part; fitted to the part, the reverse. That proved it was parallax between two depth planes, a limit of the planar model, not a bug. It led to an optional background-rejection mode that aligns the part itself, raising pixel agreement on a welded plate from 0.58 to 0.94.

To prove it works, I wrote an 84-case test suite where every synthetic case is generated from a known transformation, so the correct answer exists by construction. Only two outcomes pass: align within 5 px, or refuse cleanly. 83 of 84 pass. The suite caught two defects invisible by eye: a 320 px misalignment on a repeating grid that scored a perfect 1.00 for pixel agreement, and a regression my first fix for it introduced. The one remaining failure, at 10% overlap, is characterised and bounded.
